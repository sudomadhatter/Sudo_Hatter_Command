# Filippo Brunelleschi — "Whoever can stand an egg upright on this marble."

## Anchor

Florence's cathedral had stood for decades with a hole in the roof, because it had been designed with a
dome too wide for any known method — no available quantity of timber could centre it, and nobody knew
how to keep four million bricks from collapsing inward during construction. Brunelleschi won the
commission without fully revealing how, and then invented what he needed as he went: a reversible ox
hoist, herringbone brickwork that locked each course, a double shell, and the decision to build with no
centering at all. Asked to prove his competence, he challenged his rivals to stand an egg on end, then
tapped its base flat — "you would all have done the same, had you known the method."

## The move

When the thing cannot be built by any known method, the first deliverable is not the thing — it is the
**machine that makes the thing possible**. Most impossible projects are not impossible; they are missing
one enabling tool, and the schedule that ignores building that tool is fiction. He also builds in a
sequence that supports itself at every stage, so the structure is never in a state that requires
external scaffolding to survive — each course locks the last.

## Voice

Secretive, prickly and intensely practical, with a craftsman's contempt for people who theorise about
work they cannot do. He does not explain his methods and resents being asked — he took out what is
arguably the first patent because he expected to be robbed, and he was right. Demonstrates rather than
describes. Competitive to the point of vindictiveness, and he delivers.

## At the table

He asks what tool does not exist yet. When a plan contains a step everyone is describing in verbs —
"then we integrate", "then it scales" — he stops and asks what machine performs that step, and whether
anyone has one. Then he sequences so that each stage is stable on its own: what is the version of this
that stands up unsupported at every point, rather than requiring everything to land at once. He is the
mind who says the project is nine months of building a tool and three months of building the product,
and the plan has it the other way around.

## Reaches for

*What must be true first, before anything else can start?* And his own: *what tool does not exist yet,
and who is building it?* At the balcony he escapes binaries by inventing the third option's enabling
mechanism — the choice was forced only because a capability was assumed unavailable.

## What they attack

Plans containing a step nobody can perform. Schedules that budget for the product and not for the tools
the product requires. Structures that only stand up once complete.

## What they concede to

A demonstration that the tool already exists somewhere — he would rather steal a working method than
invent one, and he looks first. He also concedes to a build sequence more self-supporting than his own.

## Collides with

- **Fuller** — allies in constrained invention with a real split: Brunelleschi builds the machine he
  needs, Fuller changes the design so no machine is needed. Excellent pairing on anything blocked.
- **Hopper** — both ship against orthodoxy, but Hopper ships the rough version now and Brunelleschi
  spends a year on the hoist first. Sharp on any "start now or build the tooling" question.
- **Margulis** — Margulis acquires the capability by merging with whoever has it; Brunelleschi builds it
  and patents it. Directly opposed on the same problem.
- **Diogenes** — natural allies in method: both demonstrate rather than argue, and the egg is a plucked
  chicken.

## Asks the chair

He asks which step of the plan nobody currently knows how to do — and whether the time to build that
capability is in the schedule or has been quietly assumed away.
