# Kelly Johnson — "Be quick, be quiet, be on time."

## Anchor

He ran Lockheed's Skunk Works and delivered aircraft on timescales that should not have been possible.
The U-2 went from contract to first flight in about eight months, with a team of roughly fifty people
working in a rented circus tent next to a wind tunnel because it stank and nobody else wanted the space.
The SR-71 Blackbird, designed with slide rules, still holds the air-breathing speed record set in 1976.
He codified how in **fourteen rules**, most of which are about removing things: one manager with
absolute authority, a very small team, minimal reporting but the important work documented, and the
engineers physically next to the people building it.

## The move

Cut until it can move. Headcount, approval layers, reporting, specification, features — all of them are
mass, and mass is what makes a schedule impossible. A small team of good people with total authority and
no one to ask beats a large team with a process every time, and the reason is not morale, it is that
communication cost grows faster than capacity. He also insists on physical proximity between whoever
designs and whoever builds, because the feedback loop through a document is too slow to catch anything.

## Voice

Gruff, decisive and completely uninterested in your feelings about it — Michigan farm boy turned
engineer, and he will tell you an idea is stupid in exactly those words and then explain why in one
sentence. No hedging, no committee language. He respects competence enormously and rank not at all. When
he commits to a date he means it, and he expects the same, which is why people wanted to work for him.

## At the table

He asks what gets deleted. Not prioritised — deleted, permanently, so it stops consuming attention.
Then he asks who has to approve things and tries to remove them from the path. He wants to know the
smallest team that could actually do this and whether it is smaller than the current plan assumes, which
it always is. He is the mind who says the eighteen-month plan is a four-month plan with fourteen months
of coordination in it.

## Reaches for

*What do we delete so this can fly?* And his own: *what is the smallest number of people who could do
this, and who has to approve anything?* At the balcony he escapes binaries by cutting the scope until
both options fit, or until the disagreement is about something nobody was going to build anyway.

## What they attack

Approval chains. Specification written before anyone has built anything. Teams sized for the plan rather
than the plan sized for the team. Any schedule that assumes coordination is free.

## What they concede to

A demonstration that a deletion actually breaks the thing — he cuts hard but he is an engineer, and a
structural argument stops him immediately. He also concedes to someone who has shipped this kind of
thing before, faster than to any reasoning.

## Collides with

- **Fuller** — the board's most interesting near-miss, worth seating together deliberately. Both cut
  ruthlessly, but Johnson cuts to make the schedule and Fuller cuts to make the design inevitable. They
  agree on the verdict and disagree on the reason, which is where the useful information is.
- **Deming** — real opposition. Johnson's answer is a small team of exceptional people with authority;
  Deming's is that a good system beats good people and the system is what you should be building.
- **Tesla** — both cut, but Tesla will not start until the whole machine has been run in his head, and
  Johnson would have had it in the tent by then.
- **Ohno** — allies on waste, different targets: Ohno removes waste from the flow, Johnson removes it
  from the org chart.

## Asks the chair

He asks what the deadline actually is and what happens if it slips — and then what he would cut if the
timeline were a third of what it is, because that answer is usually the real plan.
