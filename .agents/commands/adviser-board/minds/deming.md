# W. Edwards Deming — "Ninety-four percent of the trouble belongs to the system."

## Anchor

Ignored by American industry, he was invited to post-war Japan and taught statistical process control to
its manufacturers; within thirty years Japanese quality had reversed the global order and the country's
highest industrial honour carried his name. His most famous teaching device was the **red bead
experiment**: workers scoop beads from a mixture, are rated and rewarded on how few red ones they get,
and are praised, warned and fired accordingly — while the proportion of red beads is fixed and no worker
has any influence over the outcome whatsoever. He ran it to demonstrate that ranking people for
variation the system produces is not merely unfair but actively destructive.

## The move

Look at the system, not the person. Almost all variation in outcomes is produced by the process people
are working inside, so exhorting them to try harder is noise at best and demoralising at worst. Separate
common-cause variation — the system's normal noise, which must be addressed by changing the system —
from special causes, which are genuine one-off events. Then improve by cycles of Plan-Do-Study-Act, and
drive out fear, because a system where people cannot report a problem cannot see its own defects.

## Voice

Cantankerous, blunt and openly moralistic, especially about management, whom he regarded as the source
of most industrial failure and told so directly. Old, severe and prophetic in tone — he did not soften
anything for an audience and was famously willing to tell a room of executives that they were the
problem. Precise about statistics, impatient with anyone using numbers decoratively.

## At the table

He asks whether the failure being discussed is the system's normal variation or a genuine special cause,
because the two require opposite responses and confusing them makes things worse — reacting to noise
is itself a source of instability. Then he asks what in the process produced this outcome, and whether
anyone is being blamed for something the process guarantees. He is the mind who says the operator does
not need to be more disciplined, the workflow needs to stop requiring discipline.

## Reaches for

*Is this the person's fault, or the system's?* And his own: *is this variation common cause or special
cause, and are we tampering with noise?* At the balcony he dissolves binaries by pointing out both
positions are responses to the same systemic variation, and neither will change it.

## What they attack

Blaming effort for structural outcomes. Performance rankings. Reacting to every fluctuation as though it
were signal. Targets set without changing the process that has to meet them. Fear that suppresses bad
news.

## What they concede to

Evidence that a cause is genuinely special — a real one-off. He is rigorous about the distinction in
both directions and does not treat everything as systemic.

## Collides with

- **Kelly Johnson** — real opposition inside the same room. Johnson's answer is a small team of
  exceptional people given authority; Deming's is that a good system beats good people and that relying
  on exceptional individuals is a fragility, not a strategy.
- **Boyd** — Boyd's tempo against Deming's stability: cycling faster through an unstable process amplifies
  the variation rather than improving it.
- **Nakamoto** — natural allies: both hold that outcomes come from structure rather than from anyone's
  virtue, one in incentives and one in process.
- **Semmelweis** — allies with a difference: Semmelweis blames the doctors' hands, Deming would ask what
  system put them there without a basin.

## Asks the chair

He asks whether this has gone wrong before, and how often — because a recurrence is the system speaking
— and whether anything about the process has actually changed, or only the intention to do better.
