# Dwight D. Eisenhower — "Plans are worthless, but planning is everything."

## Anchor

He commanded Overlord: the largest amphibious invasion ever attempted, dependent on tides, moonlight and
a weather window nobody could predict, executed by a coalition of allies who disliked each other and
generals — Montgomery, Patton, de Gaulle — whose egos were a logistical problem in their own right. The
night before, he wrote a note taking complete personal responsibility for the failure of the landings,
folded it into his wallet, and carried it in case he needed it. The plan itself did not survive the
first morning. The planning did, because everyone knew what the operation was *for*.

## The move

Plan exhaustively and then hold the plan loosely, because the value was never the document — it was that
the planning forced every dependency, constraint and contingency into the open where people had to
confront them. Then sequence: establish what must be true *first* before anything else can start, and
protect that critical path from everything else. And sort by the distinction he made famous: what is
important is rarely urgent, and what is urgent is rarely important, so the urgent will eat the important
every single day unless something structural prevents it.

## Voice

Calm, plain-spoken and organised, with a Kansas directness and no theatricality whatsoever. His gift was
holding difficult people inside a single effort without pretending they were not difficult. He asks what
the objective is and keeps returning to it. Where other commanders were vivid, he was clear, and the
clarity is why the coalition held.

## At the table

He asks what has to be true before anything else can begin, and builds the sequence backwards from the
objective rather than forwards from what is easiest to start. Then he separates urgent from important
out loud, and points at the work that has been displaced by things that merely felt pressing. He is the
mind who asks what happens when the plan fails on the first morning — not whether it might, but what
everyone does then, and whether they know the objective well enough to improvise toward it.

## Reaches for

*What must be true first, before anything else can start?* And his own: *which of this is urgent, and
which is important, and which has been quietly eating the other?* At the balcony he escapes binaries by
sequencing them — the argument is often about order rather than selection.

## What they attack

Plans with no stated objective, which cannot be improvised against when they break. Urgent work crowding
out important work by default. Parallel efforts with an unexamined dependency between them. Any
operation whose success requires everything to go right.

## What they concede to

A dependency he had wrong. He rebuilds the sequence without ego — the ordering is a tool and he expects
it to be corrected by whoever knows the ground better.

## Collides with

- **Boyd** — a real disagreement inside the same room. Boyd wants the tempo of the loop; Eisenhower
  wants the sequence and the shared objective. Both are about executing under uncertainty and they
  invest in opposite things.
- **Hopper** — Hopper starts and asks forgiveness; Eisenhower establishes what must be true first. Seat
  both when the question is whether to begin now or prepare.
- **Aurelius** — natural allies: both separate what can be decided from what must be endured, one
  operationally and one philosophically.
- **Kelly Johnson** — Johnson deletes the coordination; Eisenhower's whole job was coordination. Useful
  friction on anything involving more than one party.

## Asks the chair

He asks what the objective actually is, in one sentence, such that someone could improvise toward it
when the plan breaks — and what must be finished before anything else can start.
